The harbour town woke slowly that morning. A thin mist lay over the water, and the first ferry crept out of the basin with its lamps still lit. On the quay, a baker carried trays of warm bread to the little shop on the corner, and the smell followed her down the street like a promise. Two children argued about whose turn it was to feed the gulls. An old man sat on an overturned crate, mending a net with fingers that knew the work better than his eyes did. Nobody hurried. In a town where the tide decides when the boats can leave, people learn early that some things cannot be rushed.

By nine o'clock the mist had burned off, and the harbour showed its colours: red hulls, blue shutters, a yellow crane that had not moved in years. The harbour master climbed the stairs of his small office, checked the barometer, and wrote a few lines in a ledger that his grandfather had started. Wind from the west, light. Visibility good. Two boats expected before noon. He underlined the last line twice, because one of them belonged to his daughter, and she had promised to be back in time for lunch.

Cooking is mostly patience and attention. Take a simple soup. Start with an onion cut into small pieces and let it soften in a little oil over low heat, stirring now and then, until it turns glassy and sweet. Add garlic only at the end of that step, because garlic burns quickly and turns bitter. Then add carrots, celery and a spoonful of tomato paste, and let the paste darken for a minute so that its flavour deepens. Pour in water or stock, add a bay leaf and a pinch of salt, and let everything simmer gently. Taste it every ten minutes. When the vegetables are soft, remove the bay leaf, add pepper and a squeeze of lemon, and serve it with bread. Nothing in this recipe is difficult, but each step asks you to wait for the right moment instead of following the clock.

The history of bridges is a history of trust. The earliest bridges were fallen trees laid across a stream. Later, builders learned to stack stones into arches, because an arch turns the weight of the bridge into a push along its curve, and stone is strong when it is pressed. Roman engineers built arches that still carry visitors today. Iron and later steel allowed longer spans and slimmer shapes, and suspension bridges hung the road from cables that stretch like the strings of an enormous instrument. Every bridge is a conversation between gravity and material. The engineer's task is to make sure that gravity never wins the argument, not on the first day, not in a storm, and not a hundred years from now when nobody remembers who drew the plans.

Here is a small idea from computer science that explains a great deal. Suppose you want to find a name in a printed phone book with a thousand pages. You could start at page one and read every entry, which might take all day. Or you could open the book in the middle. If the name you want comes earlier in the alphabet, you ignore the second half; if it comes later, you ignore the first half. Then you repeat the step with the half that remains. After ten steps you are looking at a single page, because a thousand can be halved only about ten times before nothing is left. This method is called binary search, and its power comes from throwing away half of the possibilities with every question. The same trick finds a bug in a long list of changes, a word in a dictionary, and a number someone is thinking of between one and a million in just twenty guesses.

"Do you think it will rain?" asked Mira, looking up at the grey roof of the sky.

"Not before the evening," said her uncle. "The swallows are flying high, and the air smells of dust, not of wet stone. When rain is close, the swallows come low and the smell changes."

"Is that really true, or is it only something people say?"

He laughed. "A bit of both. Old sayings are often right for reasons nobody remembers. Insects fly lower when the air is damp, the birds follow the insects, and the stones give off their smell when the humidity rises. The saying is just a short way of writing down a long observation."

Mira thought about that for a while. She liked the idea that a sentence could be a way of storing many days of watching in a few words. She decided that she would keep a notebook of her own, and that the first entry would be about the swallows.

Language itself is built the same way. A word is a small container for a lot of shared experience, and a good sentence unpacks just enough of it to be understood. When we write instructions, we try to choose words that point to one clear action. When we write a story, we choose words that point to many things at once, so that the reader can bring something of their own. Both kinds of writing require the same discipline: know what you want the reader to see, and then leave out everything that does not help them see it.

Finally, a few numbers to keep the mind honest. A year has three hundred sixty-five days, except in leap years, when it has three hundred sixty-six. A day has twenty-four hours, an hour has sixty minutes, and a minute has sixty seconds. Light travels about three hundred thousand kilometres in a second, which is why a message can circle the globe in a fraction of a blink. Sound is far slower, about three hundred forty metres per second in air, which is why you see the lightning before you hear the thunder, and why counting the seconds between them tells you roughly how many kilometres away the storm is: three seconds for each kilometre.
